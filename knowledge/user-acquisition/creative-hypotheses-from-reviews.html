Building creative hypotheses from player reviews and analytics

A repeatable way to turn store reviews and audience data into creative ideas worth testing.

Most creative ideas start as a hunch. This framework grounds them in what real players say and do, so each new video has a reason to exist and a clear thing it is testing.

1. Gather two kinds of evidence

  • Who plays: age, gender and interest groups from your analytics, with engagement and revenue per user for each group. Note how much of the audience is unknown before drawing conclusions.
  • What they say: a few months of store reviews from both stores and all languages. Tag each review by theme and star rating.

2. Turn reviews into personas

Group reviewers by motivation, for example players who play to relax, players who want a brain workout, and players who were disappointed because the ad did not match the game. For each persona write down the signals (real quotes), the motivation and what it means for creative.

3. Separate what players love from what they hate

  • Love: list each insight with how many reviews mention it and how many of those are 4 to 5 stars. Rate its strength by volume.
  • Hate: list each complaint with its type (mechanic, promise, technical, content) and a clear rule for creative: be careful, or never do this.

4. Write the hypothesis table

ColumnWhat goes in it
HypothesisOne sentence about what will make the ad work.
ConceptThe concrete video or playable idea that tests it.
EvidenceThe review themes, audience data or past creative results behind it.
Promise riskHow far the ad's promise could drift from the real game, from low to high.

Group hypotheses into areas such as the core puzzle, relaxation and progression, matching the real game, and the main audience. That makes it easy to see which areas are covered.

5. Respect the promise gap

Ads that show boards, mechanics or features the game does not have can lower CPI in the short term, but they bring one-star reviews and players who churn on day one. Use a surprising hook in the first seconds if you need it, then cut to real gameplay quickly, and never promise things like "no ads" if the game shows them.

Case: a number puzzle game, 3 months of data

Here is what this process found for one live number puzzle game, using 584 store reviews (64 on the App Store with a 2.84 average, 520 on Google Play with a 4.15 average) and Firebase audience data.

FindingNumbersWhat it means for creative
Players skew female65.2% female. Revenue per user 0.315 for women vs 0.106 for menWomen are the main audience. Test hand and presenter styles that feel unisex.
Players skew older35+ is 66.4%, 45+ is 42.1%. Ages 55–64 play 6.28 sessions and earn 0.350 per user, vs 3.73 and 0.146 for ages 18–24Slower pacing, large clear numbers and big text.
Top reason to love it: brain training28 mentions, 23 of them 4–5 starsFrame the core mechanic as a logic challenge.
Second reason: relaxation22 mentions, 15 of them 4–5 starsCalm opening, no timers, finish on a cleared board.
Top complaint: in-game ads143 of 584 reviews (24.5%), and 99 of 133 one-to-two-star reviews (74.4%)Never promise "no ads" or show ad-watching in creatives.
Ad does not match game14 reviews (2.4%), 9 of them one-to-two starsShow the real board. Hooks must cut to real gameplay fast.
Bugs and crashes21 reviews, 16 of them one-to-two starsNot a creative problem. Flag to product before scaling spend.
Hooks with unusual board effectsBest ones reached D7 ROAS of 33.8% on Android and 23.7% on iOS, on small samplesStrong but risky. Keep the hook short and cut to the real board.

Note that about 48% of users had unknown age and gender, so audience splits are a direction, not a precise measure.

6. Keep a quote bank

Save strong player quotes by theme. They make great UGC scripts, end cards and moodboard references, and they keep the creative team close to the player's voice.
